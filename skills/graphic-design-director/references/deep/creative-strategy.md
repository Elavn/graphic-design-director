# Creative strategy and ideation — from brief to a memorable visual thought

Design is not applying a style to a noun. It is resolving a specific communication tension for a real audience in a real setting.

## A. Deconstruct the brief into a communication problem

Write a six-part diagnosis:
1. **Objective:** desired change in viewer knowledge, emotion, or action.
2. **Audience:** who it is for, what they currently believe, what they know already.
3. **One message:** what the work must communicate in one sentence.
4. **Evidence:** which facts, images or product properties support the claim.
5. **Context:** glance on street, pause on feed, shelf distance, editorial reading, room-scale installation.
6. **Constraint:** medium, approved identity, legal wording, visual reference, timing, budget, output size, prohibited content.

If a design must communicate five things, determine one headline proposition and organize the rest in decreasing importance. Do not use one equally weighted card for every point merely to avoid prioritizing.

## B. Convert message into concept (not adjectives)

A good **concept** creates a meaningful relationship between content and form. “Bold”, “clean”, “premium”, “editorial”, “glossy” are treatments, not concepts.

Idea-generation lenses (select based on brief):
- **Literal with a twist:** show the product directly but reveal an overlooked property.
- **Visual analogy:** map a property onto an unexpected, easily recognized object.
- **Metaphor:** make an abstract benefit physically visible, without requiring a paragraph to explain it.
- **Before/after tension:** place the problem and resolution in one coherent visual scene.
- **Scale inversion:** deliberately enlarge or miniaturize the right element to dramatize its significance.
- **Negative space:** use absence, silhouette or subtraction as part of the message.
- **Juxtaposition:** put two meanings in constructive conflict; avoid decorative cutouts with no semantic connection.
- **Typographic behavior:** have letter scale, rhythm, breaks or direction embody the message.
- **Material truth:** the substance of the subject (paper, fabric, steel, water) carries an idea through authentic texture and light.
- **Systems/seriality:** a repeated structural rule tells a story across an identity or campaign.
- **Local authenticity:** use observed local life as primary creative material, not clichés about a place or culture.

## C. Three-route divergent exercise

For an open brief, build routes that diverge at the **concept** level:

| Route | Idea | Typical form | What makes it distinct |
|---|---|---|---|
| `A: reveal` | Show an overlooked truth | close cropped photo or restrained typographic statement | unusual observation, authentic detail |
| `B: transform` | Make a benefit tactile or surprising | photomontage, surreal composite, physical prop | strong metaphor or material change |
| `C: explain` | Make a complex action immediately legible | elegant diagram, sequence, modular system | simple causal narrative |

These are thinking exercises, **not recurring composition templates**. A strong brief may demand entirely different routes. Do not force three routes for exact replication.

## D. Select the strongest route

Answer each question with evidence:
- Can the message be understood without the designer's spoken explanation?
- Is the central insight particular to this brief, or reusable for any brand by swapping logos?
- Does the idea suit actual viewing time and intended medium?
- Does it preserve required brand/subject truth?
- Can it be produced credibly with available tools and assets?
- Does it avoid appropriating the source's incidental identity or copying a famous campaign concept?
- Is there one clear image moment, or four competing metaphors?

Reject the “everything looks polished” trap: professionally polished visuals with no strategic meaning are not complete communication design.

## E. Visual thesis and proof

Use an internal sentence: **“We make [specific benefit] tangible by [visual mechanism], so [audience] understands [specific message].”**

Example, hypothetical library volunteer campaign:
- Weak: a smiling stock person with floating colorful shapes.
- Stronger: one physical stack of returned books gradually becomes a neighborhood skyline; the visual bridges individual acts and collective impact.
- Risk: copying an existing campaign's exact imagery; avoid relying on unapproved photos or unverified public claims.

## F. Know when *not* to be clever

Instructions, legal notices, safety messages, local market notices and complex charts may require utility over visual wit. The purpose is **clarity with craft**, not perpetual conceptual fireworks. A restrained, readable utility design is a valid creative decision.

## G. Originality post-check

Cover the logo and replace the company name with a generic noun. If the design still looks like it could advertise anything, diagnose whether the visual idea is too broad. If a vague abstract swirl is the only recognizable element, redesign the central idea before adding more decoration.
