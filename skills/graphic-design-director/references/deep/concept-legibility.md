# Concept legibility — imagery must express the actual idea

A strong concept is not simply a clever explanation in the prompt. The meaning must survive contact with the final image and its audience.

## Story test

1. Write a literal **viewer reading** without using the concept title: "I see [subject/action], and it suggests [idea]."
2. Identify the one visible shape or interaction that carries the thought. If it is invisible, missing, or ambiguous, the concept is unfinished.
3. Test at thumbnail size, approximate viewing distance, and with all copy hidden. Do not assume the tagline can rescue missing imagery.
4. Ask whether the visual metaphor has physical logic (light, gravity, perspective) or is intentionally surreal. A shadow shaped like something unrelated to its caster may need an explicit surreal setup.
5. Prefer one readable relationship over multiple decorative clues. Avoid unnecessary arrows, icons, UI elements and pseudo-diagrams.

## Failure diagnosis

- If the hero simply *shows a product* instead of making the insight visible, return to concept development.
- If the viewer sees "X" while the art director explains "hourglass," strengthen the hourglass's two chambers, narrow waist and context.
- If the narrative requires a paragraph to decode, consider a more direct mechanism.
- If cleverness compromises product identity or factual truth, preserve product fidelity and change the metaphor.

**Review evidence:** record the viewer's unaided reading, what object was noticed first, the intended interpretation, and the specific reason they matched or did not match. An apparent silhouette in code is not proof of conceptual clarity. Never certify these qualitative checks by measuring dimensions alone.
