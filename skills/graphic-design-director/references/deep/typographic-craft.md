# Typographic craft — micro and macro decisions

Type carries both voice and information. Every typographic setting must reflect actual reading conditions: distance, device, language, density, print technique and image contrast. These are operational design heuristics, not quotations from design books.

## 1. Build a type system before decorating

Typical roles: `DISPLAY`, `TITLE`, `DECK`, `BODY`, `CAPTION`, `UTILITY`, `LEGAL`. A short poster may need two; a report may need six. Specify each role's purpose, family/category, optical size, weight, width, case, leading, tracking and relationship to adjacent content. Do not use different fonts simply because the design needs “interest”.

## 2. Choose type for character

- **Grotesque / geometric sans:** efficient, contemporary, structural; check counters and human warmth.
- **Humanist sans:** readable, approachable, useful for dense communication.
- **Transitional or old-style serif:** conversational literary voice and typographic texture.
- **High-contrast display serif:** editorial drama at large sizes; often fragile in microcopy.
- **Monospaced / technical:** precision and labeling; use knowingly, not as a generic technology code.
- **Lettered/custom:** expressive identity or campaign title; must have a consistent internal construction principle.

Use at most two families as a *starting heuristic* rather than a prohibition. One family with multiple styles may be ideal; a rich editorial identity can need more. Confirm font availability and licensing; named fonts are not guaranteed assets.

## 3. Size and scale

Aim for a purposeful ratio rather than an arbitrary ladder. Test title-to-body contrast at real view size; extreme scale differences are valid only when the hierarchy demands them. Start body measure near 45–75 characters for long reading, adjust for font, audience and column. This range does **not** govern short display lines.

Body leading often begins near 1.25–1.5 × size, but x-height, script, line length and weight alter the correct value. Tight display leading can create visual force; ensure descenders and diacritics do not collide. Avoid tracking lowercase paragraphs for a “premium” feel.

## 4. Optical spacing, not only coordinates

- Check difficult pairs, repeated rounded forms, punctuation near edges, vertical stems and diagonal letters.
- Align round or triangular characters optically; mathematical bounding boxes do not guarantee visual alignment.
- Adjust headline line breaks for **meaning and silhouette**. Do not separate a subject from its action merely to fill a line.
- Check interword gaps, paragraph rhythm, punctuation hanging, baseline consistency and numerals in data-heavy settings.
- Keep large display words balanced: avoid one tiny dangling word or a line that forms an accidental secondary slogan.
- Consider diacritic clearance, script-specific shaping and language-sensitive capitalization; do not strip accents to make type fit.

## 5. Hierarchy tests

1. Squint test: can you see one clear dominant textual shape?
2. Blur test: does the viewer's eye move in the intended order?
3. Actual-size test: is crucial copy readable on a phone or from a few meters, as appropriate?
4. Grayscale test: do weight, size and spacing hold hierarchy independently from hue?
5. Strip test: hide supporting copy; does the headline still communicate the concept?

## 6. Corrective protocols

| Symptom | Likely cause | Targeted repair |
|---|---|---|
| Busy, no clear headline | equal text weights | create dominant display and subordinate body |
| Readable at zoom, unreadable in feed | text too small or low contrast | increase text size, simplify copy, change field |
| Headline feels “off” | line break or letterspacing | rebalance phrases, optical kern, shift anchor |
| Two expressive faces compete | conflicting voices | let one be expressive; make the other quiet |
| Body feels stiff or luxury-cliché | tracking or thin weight | reset natural spacing, choose appropriate cut |
| Text touches edges | missing safe-area system | set edge protection based on format |
| Font feels generic | untouched off-the-shelf logo letters | create systematic, minimal custom interventions |

## 7. Logo-specific lettering

A custom wordmark needs coherence across all letters: shared stroke logic, terminal language, counters, rhythm, widths and optically corrected curves. A clever isolated `p` does not justify an unrelated treatment of the other letters. Strip colors and effects; test pure one-color shapes at small and large sizes.

## 8. Common anti-patterns

Random font soups; impossible-to-read light condensed captions; artificial script used as cultural token; identical card labels everywhere; fake type on product surfaces; generated text errors disguised by image polish. Prefer deterministic text assembly when exact spelling or approved wordmarks matter.
