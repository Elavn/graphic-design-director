# Image direction: photography, illustration, CGI, mixed media and realism

Choose images by their **narrative job**, not by whether they look impressive. A product hero, a documentary portrait, a conceptual metaphor and a diagram require different photographic and visual grammars.

## 1. Visual strategy

Define subject, action, emotion, environment, era and narrative moment. Set camera: point of view, distance, focal length feel, elevation, crop, depth of field and primary light direction. Identify which details are physically necessary to maintain product truth.

For reference-based work, carry forward lens/perspective and physical characteristics only when asked. A close, slightly overhead head-on camera is not the same as a side perspective or wide-angle three-quarter product shot.

## 2. Material logic

**Metal:** reflect environment plausibly, preserve edge highlights and mechanical joints. **Painted vehicle panels:** consistent specular response across connected surfaces. **Fabric:** weave and tension follow anatomy and seams. **Paper:** thickness, folds, torn edges and realistic shadows. **Glass:** reflection and refraction can obscure logos/text. **Plastic:** controlled sheen, not a universal glossy default. **Sand/soil:** contact, weight, granularity and gravity—not arbitrary particle fireworks.

If a brief bans 3D, photography, glow, outlines, gradient or realism, treat the ban as a hard production constraint. Do not make a nominally flat illustration look CG through speculative lighting or shadows.

## 3. Physical plausibility check

Review wheels/axles and number of wheels, chassis length, headlight placement, rear volume, hand anatomy, perspective, vehicle bed opening, package scale, contact shadows and gravity. Maintain equipment category: a cargo motorcycle is not a van, box truck or pickup.

When simulating a real vehicle, use reference photographs and measurements for fabrication work; don't claim precise physical proportions from an isolated promotional image. Perspective changes **appearance**, not underlying structure.

## 4. Imagery authenticity

For local campaigns, prefer user-provided photographs, authentic locations and credible cultural context. Avoid generic “Africa” imagery, stereotyped clothing, invented landmarks or superficially diverse actors treated as decorative tokens. If source photos are unavailable, clearly label visualizations as concepts and don't imply they document a real team, customer or business.

## 5. Visual concept integration

Effective photomontage combines scale, light, camera angle, focus and shadow consistently. A surreal transformation works when the impossible element is visually believable **within the scene's internal logic**. Multiple floating app icons, severed hands, chrome spheres or useless abstract glows usually weaken the story.

## 6. Production approach

- In source-led edits, preserve what is already correct; change targeted regions rather than re-generating the entire image by default.
- Use separate layers for approved wordmarks, headlines and small exact text.
- Plan safe space for copy **before** generating the image; don't force type across busy content later.
- Recheck integrity after compositing: logos not warped, product not altered, colors role-consistent, file actually saved and sized.

## 7. Image critique table

| Symptom | Diagnosis | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Subject category changed | generator used generic archetype | reaffirm physical structure, use reference-led compositing |
| Logo looks almost right | AI approximated text | overlay approved brand file |
| Unreal reflection/shadow | conflicting light logic | re-light or retouch localized regions |
| Object looks floaty | no weight/contact | restore occlusion, contact shadow and ground plane |
| “Vintage” texture looks filtered | fake uniform grain | derive texture from actual printing/material process |
| Photo feels like a generic stock ad | no unique narrative moment | art-direct a specific action or observed real detail |
